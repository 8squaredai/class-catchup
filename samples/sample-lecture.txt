[02:05] Professor: The simplest version, the one I want you to know cold for the midterm, is CLV equals m times r, divided by one plus d minus r.
[02:20] Professor: m is the margin per customer per period. Not revenue: margin. r is the retention rate, the probability a customer stays for another period. d is the discount rate.
[02:42] Professor: This formula assumes retention is constant and the margin is constant. Keep that in mind, because in real data neither is true, and we'll come back to that.
[03:05] Professor: Let's do a quick example. Margin is fifty dollars a year. Retention is eighty percent. Discount rate is ten percent.
[03:20] Professor: So fifty times zero point eight is forty. Divided by one plus zero point one minus zero point eight, which is zero point three. Forty divided by zero point three is about one hundred thirty-three dollars.
[03:42] Professor: So each customer is worth about one hundred thirty-three dollars over their lifetime, in today's money.
[03:55] Student 2: Does that number include what we paid to acquire the customer?
[04:00] Professor: Great question. No. CLV here is the value of the customer after you've acquired them. You compare it against CAC, customer acquisition cost.
[04:15] Professor: The rule of thumb many companies use is that CLV should be at least three times CAC. If you're paying one hundred twenty dollars to acquire a customer worth one hundred thirty-three, that's a business with almost no room for error.
